Most nutrition apps will tell you, to the calorie, how much you ate. Very few can tell you whether you got enough iron this week. That is a strange gap, because for most people the second question matters more.

## Calories are the easy part

Hitting a calorie target is a solved problem. Every app does it, and for weight change it is the number that counts. Macros — protein, carbohydrate, fat — are a step harder but still routine.

What those numbers can't tell you is whether the food behind them is doing its job. Two days at 2,000 kcal and 120 g of protein can look identical on a dashboard and be nutritionally very different: one built on eggs, lentils, oily fish and leafy greens; the other on protein bars and white rice. The calorie ring is green either way.

## Where the gaps actually hide

In clinical practice, the shortfalls that come up again and again are not calories. They are a short list of micronutrients, and they cluster around predictable groups of people:

- **Iron.** The UK reference intake is 14.8 mg a day for women of reproductive age, against 8.7 mg for men — a difference most apps ignore entirely. Low iron is the most common nutritional deficiency in the world, and fatigue is often the first sign.
- **Iodine.** 140 µg a day. It comes mainly from dairy, fish and eggs, which makes it the nutrient most likely to fall short when someone moves to a plant-based diet without planning for it.
- **Vitamin D.** 10 µg a day, and very hard to reach from food alone — which is why UK guidance is that everyone consider a supplement through autumn and winter. A planner should at least show you how far food gets you.
- **Vitamin B12.** Only 1.5 µg, but it is essentially absent from plant foods, so vegans and many vegetarians need fortified foods or a supplement.
- **Calcium, zinc, folate, selenium.** Less dramatic, but each has a realistic path to falling short on a restricted or repetitive diet.

None of these show up in a calorie count. Several are the direct consequence of a diet that looks excellent on macros.

## Why most apps can't show this

It is not that developers don't care. Micronutrient analysis needs three things a calorie counter doesn't, and each one is genuinely hard.

**Ingredient-level composition data.** A calorie label on a packet gives you energy and macros. It does not give you iodine. To know what is in a meal you need a food composition database — in the UK, McCance and Widdowson's *Composition of Foods* — and you need every ingredient in a recipe matched to an entry in it, at the right weight. "Chicken" is not enough; the app has to know breast from thigh, raw from roasted.

**A reference to compare against.** A number on its own means nothing. 9 mg of iron is plenty for a man and well short for a woman. The comparison has to be against published dietary reference values, split by sex, and honest about which nutrients have an upper limit too.

**A weekly frame, not a daily one.** This is the one most often got wrong. You do not need 14.8 mg of iron every single day; you need it on average across the week. A daily view flags a "deficit" on any day you didn't eat red meat or lentils, which is most days, which trains you to ignore it. The useful question is whether the *week* got there.

## The trap that makes a bad analysis worse than none

There is a failure mode in this kind of feature that is worth knowing about, because it is invisible from the outside.

When an app cannot match an ingredient to its database — an unusual spelling, a brand name, "a handful of spinach" — the simplest thing for the code to do is count it as zero. Zero calories, zero iron, zero everything. The meal still shows a total, so nothing looks broken.

But zero is not "unknown". Zero says *this food contains no iron*. Do that to a few ingredients a week and the analysis will tell you, with total confidence, that you are short on nutrients you are actually getting. Unmeasured and deficient look identical on the chart.

## The honest limits

This is analysis of what you *planned*, from composition data, against population reference values. It is a good guide to whether a way of eating is likely to cover you. It is not a blood test, and it does not know your absorption, your medications or your history. If you have symptoms, or you are pregnant, or you have a condition that affects how you handle a nutrient, the reference values are a starting point for a conversation with a dietitian or GP, not a replacement for one.

But for the far more common situation — someone eating reasonably well, wondering whether the diet they've settled into has a hole in it — being able to see the week laid out against the numbers is the thing most apps can't offer. It was the reason BiteSync was built.
