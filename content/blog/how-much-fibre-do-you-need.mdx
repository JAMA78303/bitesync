**UK adults should aim for 30 g of fibre a day.** Children need less: about 15 g at 2–5 years, 20 g at 5–11 and 25 g at 11–16, reaching the adult 30 g by 16–18. Most people fall well short. The latest national survey found adults average about 16 g a day, and only 4 in 100 reach 30 g.

The gap is easier to close than it looks. Porridge with berries, beans on wholemeal toast and wholegrain pasta with broccoli come to about 31 g before any snacks. This guide covers where the target comes from, which foods carry the most fibre, and how to eat more without an upset stomach.

## Where the 30 g comes from

The UK's Scientific Advisory Committee on Nutrition (SACN) set the current targets in 2015, after reviewing the evidence on carbohydrates and health ([SACN, *Carbohydrates and Health*](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/sacn-carbohydrates-and-health-report)):

| Age | Fibre a day |
|---|---|
| 2–5 years | 15 g |
| 5–11 years | 20 g |
| 11–16 years | 25 g |
| 16 and over | 30 g |

The children's figures are scaled down from the adult one in line with their energy needs.

You may still see an older UK figure of 18 g. That used a narrower way of measuring fibre. On today's measure it was about 24 g, so the 2015 target was a real increase of about a quarter ([Proceedings of the Nutrition Society review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/D32A613205AE6F23509F2381379131F8/core-reader)).

## Why it matters

In the short term, fibre adds bulk to your stool and holds water in it, so it's softer and easier to pass. That helps prevent constipation.

Over the long term, eating plenty of fibre is linked to a lower risk of heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and bowel cancer ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/digestive-health/how-to-get-more-fibre-into-your-diet/)). The largest analysis, commissioned by the World Health Organization, pooled 185 cohort studies and 58 clinical trials. People who ate the most fibre had 15–30% lower rates of death from any cause and from cardiovascular disease, and of coronary heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and bowel cancer, than people who ate the least. The biggest reductions were seen at 25–29 g a day, and the dose-response data suggested that more could be better still. In the trials, eating more fibre also lowered body weight, blood pressure and total cholesterol ([Reynolds et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30638909/)).

The cohort findings are associations, so on their own they can't prove that fibre does the work. The trial results and the steady dose-response point the same way, though.

## Why most people fall short

The National Diet and Nutrition Survey measured what people across the UK ate between 2019 and 2023 ([NDNS 2019 to 2023](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/national-diet-and-nutrition-survey-2019-to-2023/national-diet-and-nutrition-survey-2019-to-2023-report)):

- **Adults** averaged 16–17 g a day. Only 4% reached 30 g.
- **11 to 18-year-olds** averaged 15.4 g. Only 4% reached their target.
- **4 to 10-year-olds** averaged 14.5 g, and 14% reached theirs.

People tend to under-record what they eat in surveys like this, so true intakes are probably a little higher. Even allowing for that, most people are well short.

Cereal foods such as bread and breakfast cereals supply 43–50% of the fibre people eat now, vegetables 19–25% and fruit 6–12%. That's why switching the bread, pasta and cereal you already eat to wholegrain versions makes the biggest single difference.

Pulses are the standout. A portion of beans or lentils has eight or nine times the fibre of a portion of apple. Weights for pasta, grains and pulses are **cooked**. [Why raw and cooked weights matter](/blog/raw-or-cooked-weight).

## How to reach 30 g

The easiest way is to change what you already eat rather than add new foods. These are the swaps the NHS suggests ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/digestive-health/how-to-get-more-fibre-into-your-diet/)):

- **Breakfast:** porridge, plain wholewheat biscuits or shredded wholegrain cereal, with fruit, nuts or seeds on top.
- **Bread, pasta and rice:** wholemeal or granary bread (or a higher-fibre white bread), wholewheat pasta, brown rice or bulgur wheat.
- **Potatoes:** keep the skins on, as baked potatoes or boiled new potatoes.
- **Pulses:** add beans, lentils or chickpeas to curries, stews and salads. Swapping some of the mince in a chilli or bolognese for lentils works well.
- **Vegetables:** have plenty with every main meal, as a side or in the sauce. Frozen veg counts.
- **Snacks:** fresh fruit, veg sticks, oatcakes, rye crackers, or unsalted nuts and seeds. Dried fruit is high in fibre but sticky, so it's kinder to your teeth eaten with a meal.

Here's a day that clears 30 g, using the values in the table above:

| Meal | What | Fibre |
|---|---|---|
| Breakfast | Porridge (45 g oats) with 80 g berries | 6.8 g |
| Lunch | 200 g baked beans on 2 slices of wholemeal toast | 14.3 g |
| Dinner | 180 g wholegrain pasta with 80 g broccoli | 9.9 g |
| Snack | 20 g almonds | 2.0 g |
| **Total** | | **33.0 g** |

## Build up slowly, and drink more

If you eat much less than 30 g now, increase over a few weeks rather than in one go. A sudden jump can cause wind, bloating and an upset stomach while your gut adjusts. Make one change at a time, such as swapping your breakfast cereal first, and let your gut settle before the next ([Worcestershire Acute Hospitals NHS Trust](https://www.worcsacute.nhs.uk/leaflets/high-fibre-diet/)).

Drink plenty as you go, because fibre holds water and works best with enough fluid. Most adults should aim for 6–8 glasses a day. Water, milk, tea and coffee all count.

## Can you have too much fibre?

For most adults, going above 30 g from everyday foods is fine, and the analysis above suggested the benefits carry on past it. Problems usually come from increasing too fast or from piling on concentrated sources.

The British Heart Foundation's dietitians warn that "fibremaxxing", loading meals with extra bran, chia, flaxseed and psyllium, can cause bloating and discomfort when taken to extremes. Raw bran and fibre powders can also reduce how much iron and calcium you absorb. A supplement can help people who struggle to eat enough fibre, but it should add to your food, not replace it ([BHF](https://bhf.org.uk/informationsupport/heart-matters-magazine/nutrition/fibre)).

## When to get individual advice

The 30 g target is for the general population. Speak to your GP or a dietitian before raising your fibre if any of these apply:

- **You have IBS.** The type of fibre matters, and some people with IBS feel better with less fibre, not more. NICE advises people with IBS to avoid insoluble fibre such as bran. If more fibre would help, it recommends soluble fibre, such as oats or ispaghula. Oats and up to a tablespoon a day of linseeds may help with wind and bloating ([NICE](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg61/chapter/1-Recommendations)).
- **You have Crohn's disease or another long-term bowel condition.** You may need advice tailored to you.
- **You're feeding young children.** Use the age targets above rather than adult amounts. A very high-fibre diet can fill children up and crowd out the protein, fat and other nutrients they need to grow ([BHF](https://bhf.org.uk/informationsupport/heart-matters-magazine/nutrition/fibre)).
- **Your bowel habits have changed.** A change that lasts several weeks, blood in your poo, losing weight without trying or ongoing tummy pain needs checking by your GP. Don't try to fix it with fibre first.
