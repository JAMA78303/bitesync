## How BiteSync checks your week

1. **Plan the week.** Place meals yourself, or let Pro fill the week. Plan all seven days: the check divides the week's total by seven, so an empty day pulls every average down.
2. **It works out what's in each meal.** Each ingredient is converted to grams and matched to a food composition table, and its vitamins and minerals are counted every time that recipe appears. Meals you mark as skipped on the Today screen drop out. If an ingredient has no vitamin and mineral data, BiteSync says how many are missing and treats the totals as a minimum, rather than counting the food as zero.
3. **It compares the daily average with reference intakes for your sex and age.** The 12 are vitamins A, C, D and B12, folate, iron, calcium, zinc, magnesium, potassium, selenium and iodine, judged against UK reference nutrient intakes. Iron, zinc, magnesium, selenium and vitamin A differ for men and women, and iron also changes with age: women need 14.8 mg a day until 50, then 8.7 mg. It also checks three limits: saturated fat (20 g a day for women, 30 g for men), salt (6 g) and free sugars (30 g, not counting whole fruit or the sugar in milk).
4. **It shows a traffic light.** Green is 90% or more of the reference, amber ("Almost there") is 70% to 90%, and red ("Low") is under 70%. A nutrient also turns amber if the week goes over a safe upper level. For the limits, amber starts at 80% and red at 100%. Results show on the planner and on the week's Nutrition page.
5. **It helps you close the gap.** A "Low" card suggests foods rich in that nutrient (for iron: lentils, spinach and oats) and can draft a snack recipe aimed at it for you to check. When you plan a new week, BiteSync looks back at your last planned week and favours recipes in your library that contain foods rich in whatever was low or almost there, since most people cook much the same recipes week to week. When you re-plan a week, it checks that week's own meals instead. The planner tells you what it leaned towards. If you haven't planned a week in the last four, it starts from a default list: vitamin D, iron, folate, vitamin C and calcium.

Calories and protein still come first: the lean decides between recipes that fit your numbers. It can only choose from recipes you have, so it narrows a gap rather than promising to close it.

## A worked example

*This is an illustration, not a real person's week. We ran an example week for a 34-year-old woman through BiteSync's own nutrition code: 28 meals and snacks, averaging about 1,650 kcal and 96 g of protein a day.*

The week: a Greek yoghurt bowl with berries and granola every morning, an apple with peanut butter every afternoon, and lunches and dinners rotating cheese sandwiches, chicken with rice and broccoli, chicken pasta, beef chilli and a chicken and sweet potato traybake.

Then we made three swaps, using foods BiteSync leans towards for this week's gaps: porridge with milk instead of yoghurt on three mornings, lentil and spinach soup instead of two cheese sandwiches, and salmon with potatoes and spinach instead of two chicken-and-rice dinners.

| Nutrient (reference for women) | Example week | After three swaps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Iron (14.8 mg) | 8.3 mg, 56%: Low | 10.8 mg, 73%: Almost there |
| Vitamin D (10 µg) | 0.5 µg, 5%: Low | 4.5 µg, 45%: Low |
| Iodine (140 µg) | 93 µg, 66%: Low | 97 µg, 69%: Low |
| Calcium (700 mg) | 602 mg, 86%: Almost there | 594 mg, 85%: Almost there |
| Potassium (3,500 mg) | 2,914 mg, 83%: Almost there | 3,730 mg, 107%: Green |

*Daily averages across the week. The other seven were green in both weeks.*

- **The same week can pass for one person and fall short for another.** Planned for a man, the first week's 8.3 mg of iron is 95% of his 8.7 mg reference: green. That's why the check needs your sex, and for iron your age: from 50, her reference would also be 8.7 mg.
- **Small swaps move some numbers a lot.** Potassium went from amber to green and iron from red to amber. Iron would need more.
- **Vitamin D stays low on food alone.** The salmon dinners lifted it to 4.5 µg a day, still under half of 10 µg, because few foods contain much. In the UK, the NHS advises everyone to consider a daily supplement containing 10 micrograms of vitamin D in autumn and winter, as it's hard to get enough from food alone ([NHS: vitamin D](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-d/)). BiteSync doesn't recommend supplements or doses beyond pointing you to that advice.
- **Iodine barely moved.** It comes mainly from dairy, fish and eggs, and these swaps mostly traded one source for another, such as milk for yoghurt.

## What the check can and can't tell you

The check compares the meals you planned with reference intakes. A low result is not a diagnosis of deficiency: it can't see food outside the plan, supplements, or how your body absorbs and stores nutrients. If you're worried about a nutrient, or have symptoms such as ongoing tiredness, speak to your GP.

BiteSync uses UK reference intakes for adults, split by sex, and for iron by age. Following the NHS, a woman's iron reference is 14.8 mg a day until 50 and 8.7 mg from 50, though women still having periods after 50 may need the higher figure ([NHS: iron](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/iron/)). The figures don't change for pregnancy or breastfeeding. If you're pregnant, breastfeeding, under 19 or have a condition that affects a nutrient, ask your GP or a dietitian which figures apply to you.

BiteSync also sets calorie and protein targets. Speak to your GP or a dietitian before following a calorie target if you're pregnant or breastfeeding, are under 18, have or have had an eating disorder, have kidney disease, or take medicine for diabetes. BiteSync is for people aged 16 and over.

## The other apps

### Cronometer: the most detailed record of what you ate

[Cronometer](https://cronometer.com/gold/) is a thorough food tracker. Its free version tracks up to 95 nutrients and other compounds from over 1 million lab-analysed or verified foods, with custom micronutrient targets and a barcode scanner. Gold ($10.99 a month or $59.99 a year) adds Oracle Nutrient Search, which suggests good food sources of a nutrient you want more of, and Repeat Items, which schedules foods into your diary. But it reports on what you've logged: it doesn't build a week of meals to your targets or make a shopping list. [BiteSync vs Cronometer](/blog/bitesync-vs-cronometer).

**When Cronometer suits you better:** if you want a nutrient-by-nutrient record of everything you eat, including food outside any plan.

### MyFitnessPal: a food log with five

[MyFitnessPal](https://www.myfitnesspal.com/premium)'s help centre lists five vitamins and minerals: vitamin A, vitamin C, calcium, iron and potassium, so not iodine, vitamin D, B12, folate or zinc. Its Meal Planner is on Premium+, its top tier: $24.99 a month or $99.99 a year in the US.

**When MyFitnessPal suits you better:** if you mainly want to log everything you eat, including packaged food by barcode.

### Eat This Much: plans to macros you set

[Eat This Much](https://help.eatthismuch.com/help/what-do-i-get-for-subscribing) generates meal plans to calorie and macro targets you set. Its food pages show vitamins and minerals, but beyond calories and macros the only targets you can set are for fibre, sodium and cholesterol. The free plan plans a day at a time; weekly plans and the grocery list are on Premium, at $5 a month with an annual membership ($59.99 a year) or $14.99 month to month.

**When Eat This Much suits you better:** if you want meals picked for you to exact macro numbers.

### Prospre: macro plans from its recipes or yours

[Prospre](https://www.prospre.io/) generates meal plans to macro ranges you set, from recipes in your Recipe Book, with a grocery list. Its food database tracks over 150 nutrients, but its homepage and pricing page don't mention checking vitamins and minerals across your week. It's free, with Premium at $19.99 a month or $99.99 a year ([Prospre pricing](https://www.prospre.io/pricing)).

**When Prospre suits you better:** if you follow your own macro split and want to set the ranges yourself.

### Samsung Food: a free recipe organiser

[Samsung Food](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/samsung-food-meal-planning/id1133637674) saves recipes from any website, plans your week and makes a shareable shopping list, free, with free daily targets and a macro breakdown of your planned meals. Samsung's website said on 11 October 2026 that Food+ tracks vitamins and minerals; its App Store listing doesn't mention them. Food+ is $6.99 a month or $59.99 a year on the US App Store.

**When Samsung Food suits you better:** if you mainly want a free place to save recipes and share a list with your household.
